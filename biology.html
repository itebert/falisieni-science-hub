Start here: the big picture

This standard is about two connected ideas:

Genetic variation
Individuals in a population have different alleles and combinations of alleles.
Inheritance
Alleles pass from parents to offspring through gametes.
Evolution
Allele frequencies in a population change over generations.
The key chain: DNA differences → different alleles → different genotypes → possible phenotype differences → differences in survival or reproduction → changing allele frequencies over generations.

In an exam, keep the levels clear: an individual has a genotype and phenotype; a population has a gene pool and allele frequencies.

1. Genetics vocabulary

These words are connected, but they do not mean the same thing.

WordSimple meaningExample
DNAThe molecule carrying inherited instructions.The biological instruction code in cells.
ChromosomeA long DNA molecule packaged with proteins; carries many genes.Humans usually have 46 chromosomes in body cells.
GeneA section of DNA that contributes to a functional product, often a protein, affecting a characteristic.A gene involved in pigment production.
AlleleA version of a gene.Different versions can contribute to different flower colours.
LocusThe position of a gene on a chromosome.The same gene occupies the same locus on homologous chromosomes.
GenotypeThe allele combination an organism carries for a gene.BB, Bb or bb.
PhenotypeAn observable characteristic produced by genotype and environment together.Flower colour or plant height.
Gene poolAll alleles present in a population.All the versions of a colour gene in a group of beetles.
Allele frequencyThe proportion of all copies of a gene that are a particular allele.0.3 or 30% of the copies are A.

Genotype descriptions

Homozygous
Two same alleles: BB or bb.
Heterozygous
Two different alleles: Bb.
Dominant
Shows in the phenotype when one copy is present, in a simple complete-dominance example.
Recessive
Shows in the phenotype only when two recessive copies are present, in a simple complete-dominance example.

A carrier is usually a heterozygous individual carrying a recessive allele without showing the recessive phenotype.

Everyday analogy: Think of a gene as a recipe category and an allele as a particular version of that recipe. The genotype is the pair of versions an organism has; the phenotype is the characteristic that results, also influenced by the environment.
Exam precision: A gene is not the same as an allele. Say “the allele for the trait” when you mean one version of a gene.

Allele frequency example

If 50 diploid individuals each have two copies of a gene, there are 100 allele copies in total. If 30 are A and 70 are a:

frequency of A = 30 ÷ 100 = 0.30 = 30%

Frequency is a proportion, not just the number of individuals with the allele.

2. Mutation: the source of new alleles

A mutation is a change in the DNA base sequence. If it changes a gene, it can create a new allele. Mutation is the original source of new alleles; meiosis reshuffles alleles that already exist.

What can cause mutations?

  • Spontaneous errors when DNA is copied.
  • Mutagens, such as ultraviolet radiation, ionising radiation and some chemicals, can increase the mutation rate.

Mutations happen randomly with respect to what an organism needs. An organism does not choose or deliberately create a useful mutation because the environment changes.

Gametic and somatic mutations

TypeWhere it happensCan offspring inherit it?
Gametic / germ-lineIn a cell that gives rise to eggs, sperm, pollen or ovules.Yes, if the altered allele is passed on at fertilisation.
SomaticIn an ordinary body cell.Generally no; it can affect that cell and its descendants in the body, but is not passed through gametes.

Possible effects

Neutral
No noticeable effect on the phenotype or reproductive success.
Harmful
Can reduce survival or reproductive success in a particular environment.
Beneficial
Can increase survival or reproductive success in a particular environment.

The effect depends on the environment. A thicker coat may help in a cold climate but be disadvantageous in a hot one.

Remember: Mutation creates new alleles. Natural selection, migration and genetic drift change how common alleles are.

3. Meiosis produces genetic variation

Meiosis is cell division that produces haploid gametes. It halves the chromosome number so fertilisation can restore the diploid number.

MitosisMeiosis
DivisionsOneTwo
Cells producedUsually twoUsually four haploid cells
Chromosome numberMaintainedHalved
Genetic resultUsually genetically very similar daughter cellsGenetically varied cells
Main roleGrowth and repairMaking gametes for sexual reproduction

Diploid (2n) means two sets of chromosomes, arranged in homologous pairs. Haploid (n) means one set. Homologous chromosomes carry the same genes in the same order, but may carry different alleles.

Three sources of variation during meiosis

1. Segregation
The two alleles of a gene separate as homologous chromosomes separate. Each gamete receives one allele of each gene.
2. Independent assortment
Different homologous chromosome pairs line up independently, creating different combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes in gametes.
3. Crossing over
Non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes exchange corresponding DNA sections in prophase I, creating recombinant chromosomes.

Variation at fertilisation

Any one sperm can fuse with any one egg. This random combination brings together alleles from two different gametes and increases variation in offspring.

Example with two genes on different chromosomes:
An individual with genotype AaBb can make AB, Ab, aB and ab gametes through segregation and independent assortment, each expected at 25% if the genes assort independently and there is no other complication.
Exam trap: Meiosis creates new combinations of existing alleles. It does not normally create new alleles; mutation does that.

4. Monohybrid inheritance and Punnett squares

A monohybrid cross follows the inheritance of one gene. A Punnett square combines possible gametes to predict possible offspring genotypes.

  1. Write both parents' genotypes.
  2. Work out the gametes. Each gamete receives one allele for the gene.
  3. Put one parent's gametes across the top and the other's down the side.
  4. Combine one allele from each gamete in every box.
  5. Count genotypes, then translate them into phenotypes.
Worked example: Bb × Bb
Assume B (black fur) is completely dominant to b (white fur). Each parent makes B and b gametes.
Bb
BBBBb
bBbbb
Genotype ratio: 1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb.
Phenotype ratio: 3 black : 1 white. Expected probability of white = 1/4 = 25%.

Useful standard crosses

CrossGenotype resultPhenotype result (complete dominance)
BB × bbAll BbAll dominant
Bb × Bb1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb3 dominant : 1 recessive
Bb × bb1 Bb : 1 bb1 dominant : 1 recessive
BB × Bb1 BB : 1 BbAll dominant

Test cross

If an organism shows the dominant phenotype, its genotype could be BB or Bb. Cross it with a homozygous recessive individual (bb). If any recessive offspring appear, the unknown parent must have supplied a b allele and is therefore Bb.

Punnett squares show probabilities, not guaranteed family sizes. Each fertilisation is an independent event. If the probability of a white offspring is 25%, that remains 25% for each new offspring.

Always label ratios. Write “3 black : 1 white”, not only “3 : 1”. If asked for phenotype, do not give only the genotype ratio.

5. Co-dominance and incomplete dominance

Sometimes the heterozygote does not look exactly like the dominant homozygote.

Incomplete dominance
The heterozygote has an intermediate or blended phenotype. Example: red snapdragon × white snapdragon can produce pink offspring.
Co-dominance
Both alleles are fully visible in the heterozygote. Example: roan cattle have both red and white hairs.
PatternHeterozygote looks likeTypical F₂ phenotype ratio from two heterozygotes
Complete dominanceDominant phenotype3 : 1
Incomplete dominanceIntermediate phenotype1 : 2 : 1
Co-dominanceBoth phenotypes expressed1 : 2 : 1

For incomplete dominance and co-dominance, the three genotypes correspond to three distinguishable phenotypes, so the phenotype ratio is often 1:2:1.

Do not confuse them: Pink flowers are a blended intermediate colour (incomplete dominance). Roan fur has separate red and white hairs visible together (co-dominance).

6. Multiple alleles and lethal alleles

Multiple alleles: ABO blood groups

A gene can have more than two allele versions in a population, even though a diploid individual carries only two copies of that gene.

For the ABO blood group system, the common alleles are Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i. Iᴬ and Iᴮ are co-dominant to each other; both are dominant over i.

GenotypeBlood group phenotype
IᴬIᴬ or IᴬiA
IᴮIᴮ or IᴮiB
IᴬIᴮAB
iiO
Example: A parent with Iᴬi can pass on Iᴬ or i. A parent with Iᴮi can pass on Iᴮ or i. Their children could have A, B, AB or O blood groups, each with a 25% expected probability.

Lethal alleles

A lethal allele causes death when a particular genotype occurs. Some lethal alleles are recessive: heterozygotes survive, but homozygotes may not. This can change the expected ratio among living offspring.

For example, if a cross produces 1 homozygous lethal : 2 heterozygous : 1 other homozygous genotype, and the lethal genotype does not survive, the living offspring ratio becomes 2 : 1.

Some lethal alleles can remain in a population because they may be hidden in healthy heterozygous carriers and passed on.

Exam method: Draw the full Punnett square first. Then identify which genotype is lethal, remove it from the living-offspring count, and calculate the ratio among survivors.

7. Dihybrid inheritance

A dihybrid cross follows two genes at the same time. If the genes are on different chromosome pairs, they can assort independently during meiosis.

Working out gametes

An individual with genotype AaBb can produce four gamete types: AB, Ab, aB and ab, assuming independent assortment. Each gamete carries one allele of each gene.

Classic cross: AaBb × AaBb
When both genes show complete dominance and assort independently, the expected phenotype ratio is:
9 : 3 : 3 : 1
9 show both dominant traits; 3 show dominant A and recessive b; 3 show recessive a and dominant B; 1 shows both recessive traits.

This ratio is not universal. It relies on both parents being heterozygous for both genes, complete dominance at each gene, and independent assortment. If genes are linked, the pattern can differ.

How to solve a dihybrid question

  1. Write each parent's genotype.
  2. List every possible gamete from each parent.
  3. Use a 4 × 4 Punnett square when each parent has four gamete types.
  4. Combine alleles for both genes in each box.
  5. Group the offspring into the phenotype categories asked for.
  6. Give the ratio, fraction or percentage requested, with labels.
Do not automatically write 9:3:3:1. First check the parental genotypes, dominance pattern and whether the genes are independently assorting.

8. Pedigree charts

A pedigree is a family diagram used to track how a characteristic is inherited over generations.

SymbolMeaning
SquareMale
CircleFemale
Shaded symbolIndividual shows the characteristic
Unshaded symbolIndividual does not show the characteristic
Horizontal lineParents / mating pair
Vertical lineConnects parents to offspring

Clues to inheritance patterns

  • Dominant: often appears in every generation; an affected individual usually has an affected parent (allowing for rare new mutations and incomplete information).
  • Recessive: can skip generations; two unaffected carrier parents can have an affected child.
  • Autosomal: gene is on a non-sex chromosome; males and females can be affected at similar rates.
  • X-linked recessive: often more males are affected; there is no father-to-son transmission because a father gives his Y chromosome to sons.

Working method

  1. Identify who is affected and unaffected.
  2. Check whether unaffected parents have an affected child; this supports recessive inheritance.
  3. Check whether father-to-son transmission occurs; this rules out X-linkage.
  4. Assign certain genotypes first, then work out the remaining possible genotypes.
  5. If more than one genotype is possible, say so rather than guessing.
  6. Use a Punnett square to calculate a requested offspring probability.
Be careful: A pedigree may not contain enough information to decide every genotype. State what is certain and what remains possible.

9. Linked genes and crossing over

Linked genes are genes located on the same chromosome. They tend to be inherited together, so they do not assort independently as often as genes on different chromosomes.

Parental and recombinant combinations

Parental types have the same allele combinations as the original parents. Recombinant types have new combinations, produced when crossing over exchanges sections between homologous chromosomes.

Crossing over happens during prophase I of meiosis. Genes that are close together are less likely to have a crossover occur between them; genes farther apart are more likely to be separated by crossing over.

Recombination frequency

recombination frequency = (number of recombinant offspring ÷ total offspring) × 100%

Worked example:
There are 1000 offspring, and 180 are recombinant types.
Recombination frequency = 180 ÷ 1000 × 100 = 18%.
This suggests the genes are linked; a lower recombination percentage generally indicates closer genes.

In a simple mapping model, 1% recombination is often treated as approximately 1 map unit (1 centimorgan), up to a maximum observed frequency of about 50%.

Exam explanation: If parental types are much more common than recombinant types, explain that the genes are linked on the same chromosome and crossing over only sometimes separates them.

10. Sex linkage

In humans, most females have XX sex chromosomes and most males have XY. An X-linked gene is located on the X chromosome. The Y chromosome does not carry a matching copy of many X-linked genes.

A male has only one X chromosome, so a recessive allele on that X can be expressed because there may be no corresponding dominant allele on the Y. This is why X-linked recessive characteristics are often more common in males.

Writing X-linked genotypes

Use superscripts to show the allele on the X chromosome. For example, XᴺXⁿ can represent a heterozygous carrier female; XⁿY can represent an affected male for a recessive trait.

Carrier mother × unaffected father

Mother: XᴺXⁿ. Father: XᴺY.

Father XᴺFather Y
Mother XᴺXᴺXᴺ — unaffected daughterXᴺY — unaffected son
Mother XⁿXᴺXⁿ — carrier daughterXⁿY — affected son

Each box has a 25% probability for all children in this simplified cross. Among sons, half are expected to be affected; among daughters, half are expected to be carriers. No son inherits his father's X chromosome; sons receive their father's Y.

Do not say “fathers pass X-linked alleles to sons”. A father passes his Y chromosome to a son and his X chromosome to a daughter.

11. Natural selection

Natural selection occurs when individuals with inherited phenotypes that are better suited to the current environment tend to survive and reproduce more successfully. They pass their alleles to more offspring, so allele frequencies can change over generations.

The process in four steps

  1. Variation: individuals in the population already differ. Some variation is inherited and comes from different alleles.
  2. Selection pressure: an environmental factor affects survival or reproductive success, such as predators, disease, climate or competition.
  3. Different reproductive success: individuals with a favourable inherited phenotype tend to leave more offspring.
  4. Change in the gene pool: alleles associated with that phenotype can become more common over generations.
Everyday example: beetle colour
Imagine a population with light and dark beetles living on dark tree bark. Birds more easily spot the light beetles. Dark beetles are more likely to survive and reproduce, so the dark-colour allele may increase in frequency over generations.

Natural selection acts on the phenotype of individuals, but evolution is described as a change in allele frequencies in the population. Individuals do not evolve genetically during their own lifetime; populations change over generations.

Selection pressures and mate choice

Selection pressures include predators, parasites, disease, temperature, food availability and competition. Mate choice can also affect reproductive success; this is sexual selection.

Natural selection does not create the useful allele and does not give organisms traits because they need them. Mutation supplies new alleles; selection changes how common inherited variants become.

12. Migration and gene flow

Migration can change a population's gene pool when individuals move into or out of a population and then breed. The movement of alleles between populations is called gene flow.

Immigration
Individuals enter a population and may bring alleles that were rare or absent.
Emigration
Individuals leave and take their alleles with them, potentially changing allele frequencies in the population they leave.

For migration to change the next generation's gene pool, migrants need to reproduce. If they arrive but do not breed, their alleles are not added to the breeding population's next generation.

Example: A few birds from a nearby island join another island's population and breed. Their alleles become part of the recipient population's gene pool.

Gene flow can make populations more genetically similar. Habitat fragmentation can reduce movement and breeding between groups, limiting gene flow.

13. Genetic drift, bottlenecks and founder effect

Genetic drift is a random change in allele frequencies due to chance. It has a stronger effect in small populations. Unlike natural selection, drift does not happen because an allele is more useful.

Genetic bottleneck

A bottleneck occurs when a population is suddenly reduced to a much smaller size, for example by a storm, disease outbreak or habitat loss. The survivors are a random sample of the original gene pool. Some alleles may be lost by chance, so genetic variation is reduced.

Founder effect

The founder effect occurs when a small number of individuals start a new population. Their alleles are only a sample of the original population, so the new population may have different allele frequencies and lower variation.

ProcessWhat happens?
BottleneckA large population is drastically reduced; survivors form the smaller gene pool.
Founder effectA few individuals establish a new population elsewhere.

Why low genetic variation matters

  • There may be fewer alleles that help individuals cope with new diseases or environmental changes.
  • Inbreeding is more likely in small, isolated populations, increasing the chance that harmful recessive alleles are paired together.
  • Even if population numbers later increase, lost alleles do not automatically return. New mutation or gene flow may add variation over time.
Everyday analogy: Imagine a jar containing many colours of beads. If only a handful are randomly scooped out to start a new jar, the new jar may have very different colour proportions and may contain none of some colours. That is the basic idea of founder effect.
Drift vs selection: Natural selection is non-random with respect to inherited differences in reproductive success. Genetic drift is change by chance, especially noticeable in small populations.
