How to use this page

Big idea: A cell is a tiny living system. It needs materials, removes waste, transfers energy and makes new cells.

1. Cells and the movement of materials

Cell structures and their jobs

Cells are the basic units of life. Different parts of a cell have different jobs. These specialised parts are called organelles.

Plant cells usually have a cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts in photosynthetic parts, and a large permanent vacuole. Animal cells do not have these structures. Cells can also become specialised: for example, a red blood cell is adapted to carry oxygen, while a root hair cell has a long extension that helps absorb water and mineral ions.

Cell size and surface area to volume ratio

The surface area is the outside area of a cell. The volume is the space inside it. Materials enter and leave across the cell membrane, so surface area affects how much exchange can happen. The cell's volume affects how much material it needs.

Surface area to volume ratio = surface area ÷ volume
Everyday example: Imagine two rooms with one doorway each. A small room has less space needing fresh air. A much larger room has more space to supply through a doorway. Cells face a similar exchange challenge as they grow.

As a cell gets bigger, its volume increases faster than its surface area. This means its surface area to volume ratio becomes smaller. A smaller ratio makes it harder for the cell to bring in enough nutrients and oxygen and remove wastes quickly enough. This is one reason cells are usually small.

Worked cube example

For a cube, surface area = 6 × side² and volume = side³.

Cube sideSurface areaVolumeSA:V ratio
20 μm6 × 20² = 2,400 μm²20³ = 8,000 μm³2,400 ÷ 8,000 = 0.3:1
60 μm6 × 60² = 21,600 μm²60³ = 216,000 μm³21,600 ÷ 216,000 = 0.1:1
Exam tip: Do not just say “bigger cells are less efficient”. Explain that volume increases faster than surface area, so there is less membrane area available per unit of cell volume for exchange.

Three ways substances cross membranes

ProcessDirectionEnergy needed?What happens?
DiffusionHigh concentration → low concentrationNoParticles spread out down their concentration gradient.
OsmosisWater moves from a dilute solution to a more concentrated solutionNoWater crosses a partially permeable membrane.
Active transportCan move low concentration → high concentrationYes, ATP is usedCarrier proteins move substances against their concentration gradient.

Facilitated diffusion is passive movement down a concentration gradient through a membrane protein. It does not use ATP. It helps substances that cannot easily pass through the phospholipid bilayer on their own, such as some ions or glucose.

Diffusion example: When someone sprays perfume in one part of a room, scent particles spread from the area where they are concentrated to areas where fewer particles are present.
Osmosis example: Think of water moving through a selective barrier. Water moves across the membrane towards the side with the higher solute concentration (the lower water concentration).

What happens to cells in different solutions?

  • Animal cell in a dilute solution: water enters by osmosis; the cell may swell and burst because it has no cell wall.
  • Animal cell in a concentrated solution: water leaves by osmosis; the cell shrinks.
  • Plant cell in a dilute solution: water enters; the vacuole expands and the cell becomes turgid. The cell wall prevents it from bursting easily.
  • Plant cell in a concentrated solution: water leaves; the cell membrane pulls away from the cell wall. This is called plasmolysis.

A potato osmosis investigation can compare potato pieces placed in solutions of different concentrations. Measure their mass before and after. A gain in mass suggests water entered the potato cells; a loss suggests water left them. Keep the potato size, time, temperature and solution volume consistent for a fair test.

Active transport

Active transport moves substances against their concentration gradient, from a lower concentration to a higher concentration. It requires energy from ATP and uses carrier proteins in the membrane. For example, root hair cells can absorb mineral ions from soil even when the concentration of those ions is lower in the soil than inside the cell.

How to identify active transport: Look for movement against the concentration gradient, use of ATP, and membrane carrier proteins. Oxygen may be needed indirectly because aerobic respiration helps produce ATP, but oxygen itself is not the energy source used by the transport protein.

2. Enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts. They speed up chemical reactions in living things without being used up by the reaction.

How an enzyme works

  • A substrate is the substance an enzyme acts on.
  • The substrate fits into the enzyme's active site.
  • An enzyme-substrate complex forms.
  • The reaction happens and product(s) are released.
  • The enzyme can be used again.
Everyday example: Think of an enzyme as a tool designed for a particular job. A screwdriver works best with a matching screw; an enzyme's active site works with particular substrate(s).

Enzymes are specific because the shape and chemical properties of the active site suit particular substrates. The lock-and-key model describes a substrate fitting an active site with a matching shape. The induced-fit model adds that the active site can change shape slightly as the substrate binds.

Enzymes lower the activation energy needed for a reaction to begin. They do not change the overall energy released or absorbed by the reaction.

Factors affecting enzyme activity

Temperature

As temperature rises, particles move faster and collisions become more frequent, so activity usually increases up to an optimum. Above the optimum, the enzyme may denature.

pH

Each enzyme has an optimum pH. Too acidic or too alkaline conditions can change the active site's shape and reduce activity.

Substrate concentration

More substrate can increase reaction rate until the enzyme active sites are mostly occupied. The rate then levels off.

Co-factors and co-enzymes

Some enzymes need helper substances to work. A co-factor is often an inorganic ion; a co-enzyme is an organic helper molecule.

Inhibitors

Inhibitors reduce enzyme activity. Some block the active site; others bind elsewhere and alter its shape.

Denaturation is a change in an enzyme's three-dimensional shape. If the active site changes shape, the substrate may no longer fit, so the enzyme cannot work properly. High temperatures and extreme pH can cause denaturation.

Graph tip: For an enzyme-temperature graph, describe the rise to the optimum and the sharp fall after it. Explain the fall using denaturation. For a substrate-concentration graph, explain the plateau using saturation of active sites.

3. Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is the process plants use to convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. It occurs in chloroplasts, which contain the green pigment chlorophyll.

Carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen

Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen. Light energy is required. The balanced symbol equation is:

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

How materials reach the leaf

  • Carbon dioxide enters the leaf through tiny pores called stomata.
  • Water is absorbed by roots and carried to leaves through xylem vessels.
  • Light is absorbed by chlorophyll in chloroplasts.

Plants use glucose for respiration, to make cellulose for cell walls, to make other organic molecules, or convert it into starch for storage. Oxygen can diffuse out through the stomata.

Two stages of photosynthesis

Light-dependent reactions

Occur in the thylakoid membranes. Light energy is absorbed. Water is split, oxygen is released, and energy-carrying molecules are produced for the next stage.

Light-independent reactions (Calvin cycle)

Occur in the stroma. Carbon dioxide is used with energy from the light-dependent stage to build carbohydrate molecules.

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration are linked. Photosynthesis stores energy in glucose and releases oxygen. Aerobic respiration uses glucose and oxygen to transfer energy into ATP, releasing carbon dioxide and water.

Factors that limit the rate

  • Light intensity: increasing light can increase the rate until another factor becomes limiting.
  • Carbon dioxide concentration: more carbon dioxide can increase the rate until the system reaches a limit.
  • Temperature: photosynthesis involves enzyme-controlled reactions, so a temperature change can affect the rate. Very high temperatures can damage enzymes.
Graph tip: If a graph levels off, explain that another factor has become limiting. Do not claim that increasing one factor will keep increasing the rate forever.

4. Cell respiration

Cellular respiration is a set of reactions that transfers energy from glucose into ATP. ATP is the cell's immediate energy currency. Cells use it for processes such as active transport, movement and building molecules.

Aerobic respiration

Aerobic respiration uses oxygen to break down glucose and transfers a relatively large amount of energy into ATP. Most of the stages after glycolysis occur in the mitochondria.

Glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy (ATP)
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O

Anaerobic respiration in human muscle

When muscle cells cannot receive oxygen quickly enough for the energy demand, they can make ATP through anaerobic glycolysis. This produces much less ATP per glucose molecule than aerobic respiration. In human muscles, lactate is produced.

AerobicAnaerobic (human muscle)
Requires oxygenDoes not require oxygen for glycolysis
High ATP yield per glucoseLow ATP yield per glucose
Carbon dioxide and water are final products overallLactate is produced
Can support longer-duration energy needsCan supply ATP quickly for short periods of high demand

After intense exercise, breathing and heart rate may remain elevated. This helps deliver oxygen and restore the body's conditions. Lactate can be transported and used by the body; it is not simply a waste product that stays in muscles forever.

Everyday example: Think of aerobic respiration as a more efficient way of getting energy from fuel when oxygen supply meets demand. Anaerobic glycolysis is a quicker back-up pathway that produces less ATP from each glucose molecule.
Exam tip: Explain the difference, not just name it: aerobic respiration uses oxygen and produces much more ATP per glucose, while anaerobic glycolysis in muscles does not use oxygen and produces lactate with a much smaller ATP yield.

5. Cell division

The cell cycle

The cell cycle is the sequence of growth and division a cell goes through. Before a cell divides, it grows and copies its DNA during interphase. The cell then divides its nucleus by mitosis and separates into two daughter cells during cytokinesis.

  • G₁: the cell grows and carries out its normal activities.
  • S phase: DNA is replicated (copied).
  • G₂: the cell grows further and prepares for division.
  • M phase: mitosis separates the copied chromosomes; cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm.

DNA structure and replication

DNA is a double helix made of two strands. Each strand has a sugar-phosphate backbone and bases. The four bases are adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G). Complementary base pairing means A pairs with T, and C pairs with G.

Base-pair rule: A–T and C–G. If one strand has the sequence A T C G, its complementary strand is T A G C.

The two DNA strands run in opposite directions; this is described as antiparallel. During replication, the strands separate and each original strand acts as a template for a new complementary strand. The result is two DNA molecules, each containing one original strand and one newly made strand. This is called semi-conservative replication.

Hydrogen bonds

A–T base pairs have 2 hydrogen bonds, while C–G base pairs have 3. To calculate the total number of hydrogen bonds, count each type of base pair and multiply by its bond number, then add the results.

Example: If a short DNA section has 4 A–T pairs and 3 C–G pairs, the total is (4 × 2) + (3 × 3) = 8 + 9 = 17 hydrogen bonds.

Mitosis

Mitosis produces two daughter nuclei with the same chromosome number and (normally) the same genetic information as the original nucleus. It is important for growth, repair and replacement of cells, and asexual reproduction in some organisms.

  1. Prophase: chromosomes condense and become visible; the nuclear envelope breaks down and the spindle forms.
  2. Metaphase: chromosomes line up along the middle of the cell.
  3. Anaphase: sister chromatids separate and are pulled to opposite poles.
  4. Telophase: new nuclear envelopes form around each set of chromosomes.
  5. Cytokinesis: the cytoplasm divides, forming two daughter cells.

Cell-cycle control and cancer

Cells have control systems, including checkpoints, that help ensure DNA is copied correctly and division happens at the right time. If changes (mutations) affect genes that control the cell cycle, cells may divide when they should not. A mass of abnormal cells can form a tumour. Cancer involves uncontrolled cell division and can spread to other parts of the body in some cases.

Exam tip: When explaining why mitosis matters, link the process to the outcome: it makes genetically similar cells, allowing an organism to grow and replace or repair damaged cells.
