Start here: the big picture

Almost everything in this topic comes back to one question: what particles are present, how are they held together, and how much energy is needed to change them?

Bonding
How atoms or ions are held together.
Structure
How the particles are arranged.
Properties
How a substance behaves because of its structure.
Energy
Energy absorbed or released when bonds or attractions change.
Exam habit: Do not just name a property. Explain it using the particles and attractions involved. For example: “It conducts because delocalised electrons can move through the metal and carry charge.”

1. The three types of bonding

Ionic bonding — electrons are transferred

Ionic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between positive and negative ions. It usually forms between a metal and a non-metal. The metal loses electrons and becomes a positive ion (cation). The non-metal gains electrons and becomes a negative ion (anion).

Everyday example: table salt (sodium chloride)
Sodium gives one electron to chlorine. Sodium becomes Na⁺ and chlorine becomes Cl⁻. Opposite charges attract, holding the ions together.

Group patterns help predict common ion charges:

Periodic table groupCommon ionReason
11+Loses 1 electron
22+Loses 2 electrons
133+Loses 3 electrons
162−Gains 2 electrons
171−Gains 1 electron

Writing ionic formulae

The total positive charge and total negative charge must balance, making the compound neutral.

  1. Write the ion and its charge.
  2. Choose the smallest whole-number ratio that balances the charges.
  3. Write the positive ion first and use subscripts for the ratio.
Example: magnesium chloride
Mg²⁺ needs two Cl⁻ ions to balance the +2 charge. Formula: MgCl₂.

Ionic compounds form a giant ionic lattice, a repeating 3D arrangement of positive and negative ions. Each ion is attracted to several oppositely charged ions, not just one partner.

Covalent bonding — atoms share electrons

Covalent bonding usually occurs between non-metals. Two atoms share one or more pairs of outer-shell electrons. The shared pair is attracted to both positive nuclei, holding the atoms together.

Single bond
1 shared pair · H—H
Double bond
2 shared pairs · O=O
Triple bond
3 shared pairs · N≡N

A molecule is a group of atoms joined by covalent bonds. The bonds inside a molecule are strong; attractions between separate molecules are a different thing.

Metallic bonding — delocalised electrons

Metallic bonding is the attraction between a lattice of positive metal ions and a sea of delocalised electrons. Delocalised means the electrons are not attached to one particular atom and can move through the structure.

Everyday example: copper wire
Mobile electrons carry electrical charge through copper. Layers of metal ions can slide while the electron sea continues holding the structure together, allowing copper to be drawn into wires.

Compare the three

BondingParticlesWhat holds them together?
IonicPositive and negative ionsElectrostatic attraction between opposite charges
CovalentAtoms in molecules or networksShared pairs of electrons attracted to both nuclei
MetallicPositive metal ions and delocalised electronsAttraction between ions and the electron sea
Common exam trap: In a solid metal, electrons move and carry charge; the positive metal ions stay in fixed positions. In molten ionic compounds, the ions themselves can move.

2. Lewis structures

A Lewis structure shows the outer-shell (valence) electrons in a molecule. A line represents one shared pair of electrons. Dots represent lone pairs — pairs that belong to one atom and are not shared.

How many outer electrons?

1H1
13B3
14C, Si4
15N, P5
16O, S6
17F, Cl, Br, I7

Drawing a simple Lewis structure

  1. Add up the outer electrons from every atom.
  2. Choose the central atom. Hydrogen is never central because it forms only one bond.
  3. Join the central atom to the outside atoms with single bonds. Each bond uses two electrons.
  4. Place the remaining electrons as lone pairs, filling the outside atoms first.
  5. Check the electron count around each atom: usually 8 for C, N, O and halogens; 2 for H.
  6. If the central atom is short of an octet, a lone pair may need to become a shared pair, making a double or triple bond.
Example: water, H₂O
Oxygen has 6 outer electrons and each hydrogen has 1, giving 8 in total. Oxygen forms two O—H single bonds and keeps two lone pairs. Each hydrogen has 2 electrons; oxygen has 8 around it.
Example: carbon dioxide, CO₂
Carbon has 4 outer electrons and each oxygen has 6, giving 16. The final structure is O=C=O. Carbon forms two double bonds, and each oxygen has two lone pairs.
Remember boron: In molecules such as BF₃, boron can have six electrons around it. Do not automatically force every atom to have eight.

3. Molecular shape and polarity

Why molecules have shapes

Electron regions repel one another. They arrange themselves as far apart as possible. This is the idea behind VSEPR (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion) theory.

Important: A double bond counts as one region of electron density when predicting shape. Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs, so they can squeeze bond angles smaller.

Electron regions around centreBonding pairs / lone pairsShapeTypical angleExample

Bond polarity vs molecular polarity

A bond is polar when electrons are shared unequally because one atom attracts the shared electrons more strongly. This creates a slightly negative end (δ−) and a slightly positive end (δ+).

To decide whether the whole molecule is polar, consider both the polar bonds and the molecule's shape. The bond dipoles may cancel or may not cancel.

CO₂ is non-polar overall
Each C=O bond is polar, but the molecule is linear and symmetrical. The pulls cancel.
NH₃ is polar
The molecule is trigonal pyramidal. Its bond dipoles do not cancel, leaving an overall dipole.
Exam sentence: “The bonds are polar, but the molecule is symmetrical, so the bond dipoles cancel and the molecule is non-polar.” Only use this when the molecule really is symmetrical.

4. Forces between molecules

In a molecular substance, covalent bonds hold atoms together inside each molecule. Intermolecular forces attract separate molecules to one another.

ForceWhat it meansUseful detail
London dispersion forcesTemporary uneven electron distributions create temporary attractions.Present in all atoms and molecules; generally stronger with more electrons and greater contact area.
Dipole–dipole forcesAttraction between the positive end of one polar molecule and the negative end of another.Occurs between polar molecules.
Hydrogen bondingA particularly strong type of dipole attraction involving H bonded to N, O or F and a lone pair on N, O or F nearby.Important in water, ammonia and hydrogen fluoride.

When a simple molecular substance melts or boils, the intermolecular forces are overcome. The covalent bonds inside the molecules are not broken during an ordinary change of state.

Everyday example: boiling water
Water molecules separate further from one another as water boils. The O—H covalent bonds inside each H₂O molecule remain intact.

Stronger intermolecular forces generally mean more energy is needed to separate molecules, so the substance has a higher boiling point.

5. Structure and properties

Four broad structure types help explain melting point, conductivity, hardness and solubility.

StructureParticles and attractionsTypical properties
Simple molecularSmall molecules; intermolecular forces between moleculesOften low melting/boiling points; usually do not conduct electricity
Ionic latticePositive and negative ions in a giant latticeUsually high melting points; brittle; conduct when molten or dissolved, not as solids
Metallic latticePositive metal ions and delocalised electronsConduct as solids and liquids; malleable and ductile
Covalent networkAtoms joined by covalent bonds in a giant networkUsually very high melting points; often hard; generally do not conduct (graphite is an important exception)

Melting and boiling points

Ask: what attractions must be overcome to change the state? For simple molecular substances, it is the intermolecular forces. For ionic lattices, it is the attractions between ions. For metals, it is metallic bonding. For covalent networks, many strong covalent bonds must be disrupted.

Within a similar type of substance, stronger attractions usually mean a higher melting or boiling point. For molecular substances, larger electron clouds often mean stronger dispersion forces. Molecular shape and hydrogen bonding can also matter.

Electrical conductivity

  • Metals: conduct because delocalised electrons can move through the structure.
  • Solid ionic compounds: do not conduct because ions are locked in place.
  • Molten or dissolved ionic compounds: conduct because ions can move and carry charge.
  • Most molecular substances: do not conduct because they do not have mobile charged particles.
  • Covalent networks: usually do not conduct; graphite is a notable exception because it has delocalised electrons.

Hardness, malleability and ductility

Hardness is resistance to scratching or indentation. Malleability means a material can be hammered into sheets. Ductility means it can be drawn into wires.

Metals are malleable and ductile because layers of positive ions can slide while the electron sea continues attracting them. Ionic solids are brittle: when layers shift, like charges can line up, repel and split the crystal.

Solubility: like dissolves like

Water is polar. Many non-polar solvents, such as hexane, are non-polar. A useful guide is like dissolves like: polar substances are more likely to dissolve in polar solvents, while non-polar substances are more likely to dissolve in non-polar solvents.

Examples
Salt can dissolve in water because polar water molecules attract and surround the ions. Iodine is non-polar, so it dissolves better in a non-polar solvent than in water.
“Like dissolves like” is a guide, not a magic rule. Solubility depends on whether the new attractions formed are favourable enough to replace the attractions already present.

6. Energy changes: exothermic and endothermic

Enthalpy change (ΔrH) describes the heat energy change for a reaction at constant pressure, as used in this course.

Exothermic
Energy is released to the surroundings. The surroundings warm up. ΔrH is negative.
Endothermic
Energy is absorbed from the surroundings. The surroundings cool down. ΔrH is positive.

On an enthalpy diagram, reactants and products are shown at different energy levels. For an exothermic reaction, products are lower than reactants. For an endothermic reaction, products are higher.

Everyday examples
Many hand warmers release heat (exothermic). Some instant cold packs absorb heat from their surroundings (endothermic).

The activation energy is the energy needed to get a reaction started. It is shown as the energy rise from the reactants to the top of the energy profile. A catalyst lowers the activation energy but does not change the overall ΔrH.

7. Enthalpy calculations and moles

The ΔrH value belongs to the balanced chemical equation as written. It refers to the amounts shown by the equation's coefficients.

  1. Write the balanced equation and identify what amount the ΔrH refers to.
  2. Convert the given mass to moles using n = m ÷ M.
  3. Use the mole ratio from the balanced equation if the substance is not the one directly linked to ΔrH.
  4. Scale the enthalpy change in the same ratio.
  5. Keep the sign: negative means released; positive means absorbed.
Example idea
If the equation releases 200 kJ when 2 mol of a fuel reacts, then 1 mol releases 100 kJ. If 4 mol reacts, the energy change is twice the equation amount. Always show the mole ratio and units.
Units check: Molar enthalpy is commonly written in kJ mol⁻¹. Total energy is written in kJ. Do not mix them up.

8. Energy and changes of state

Changing state also involves energy because particles must change how strongly they are held near one another.

ChangeEnergy direction
Melting (solid → liquid)Energy absorbed
Boiling / evaporation (liquid → gas)Energy absorbed
Sublimation (solid → gas)Energy absorbed
Freezing (liquid → solid)Energy released
Condensation (gas → liquid)Energy released
Deposition (gas → solid)Energy released

On a heating curve, sloping sections show temperature increasing. Flat sections show a change of state: energy is being used to overcome attractions rather than increase the particles' average kinetic energy, so the temperature stays constant during the change.

Boiling usually requires more energy than melting because particles must separate much further to become a gas.

9. Bond enthalpy calculations

Breaking bonds requires energy. Making bonds releases energy. The overall reaction energy depends on the balance between these two processes.

Key relationship:
ΔH = energy to break bonds − energy released making bonds
  1. List every bond broken in the reactants.
  2. Count how many of each bond type are present.
  3. Multiply each bond count by its bond enthalpy and add them: this is energy in.
  4. List and count every bond formed in the products.
  5. Multiply each product bond count by its bond enthalpy and add them: this is energy released.
  6. Calculate broken − formed. A negative answer is exothermic; a positive answer is endothermic.
Small example
If breaking the reactant bonds takes 500 kJ mol⁻¹ and forming the product bonds releases 650 kJ mol⁻¹, then ΔH = 500 − 650 = −150 kJ mol⁻¹. The reaction is exothermic.
Common mistake: Do not reverse the subtraction. It is always energy to break bonds minus energy released when bonds form.
