Part 01 · Static electricity

Electric fields between parallel plates

What is an electric field?

An electric field is a region where a charged object experiences an electric force. Field direction is defined as the direction a positive test charge would be pushed. So field lines leave a positive plate and point towards a negative plate.

Between two large, parallel plates, the field in the central region is approximately uniform: its strength and direction stay the same. Draw straight, parallel, evenly spaced lines at right angles to the plates. Lines near the outer edges curve slightly.

Field strength

E = V ÷ d
  • E = electric field strength (V m−1 or N C−1)
  • V = potential difference between plates (V)
  • d = separation of plates (m)

A larger voltage makes the field stronger. A larger gap makes it weaker if the voltage stays the same. Moving a charge around inside a uniform field does not change the field strength: the plates create the field.

Worked example. Plates are 4.0 mm apart with 200 V across them.
Convert: d = 4.0 × 10−3 m.
E = 200 ÷ (4.0 × 10−3) = 5.0 × 104 V m−1.
Direction: from the positive plate to the negative plate.

Force on a charge

F = qE

F is force in newtons, q is charge in coulombs, and E is field strength. A positive charge is forced along the field; a negative charge is forced opposite the field. The force magnitude increases if either the charge magnitude or field strength increases.

Worked example. A +3.0 µC charge is placed in a field of 5.0 × 104 N C−1.
q = 3.0 × 10−6 C.
F = qE = (3.0 × 10−6)(5.0 × 104) = 0.15 N, towards the negative plate.

The elementary charge has magnitude 1.6 × 10−19 C. A proton is positive and an electron is negative. For tiny particles, electric force is usually much larger than their weight.

Work and electric potential energy

ΔEp = qEd    and    ΔEp = qV

Use qEd when the charge moves a known distance along the field. Use qV when it moves through the full potential difference. A sideways movement at right angles to the field transfers no energy through the electric force.

When a charge moves in the direction of its electric force, electric potential energy decreases and kinetic energy increases. Moving it against the force requires external work and increases its electric potential energy.

Voltage is energy transferred per coulomb: V = ΔE ÷ q. One volt equals one joule per coulomb.

Charged particles gaining speed

qV = ½mv²    so    v = √(2qV ÷ m)

This energy method is usually quickest when a particle starts from rest and the question asks for its final speed. If it starts with an initial speed, use qV = ½m(vf2 − vi2).

If a particle enters the field moving sideways, it has constant velocity in its original direction and constant acceleration in the field direction. These motions happen at the same time, producing a curved, parabolic path.

Part 02 · Direct current

Charge, current and voltage

Current describes how quickly charge passes a point in a circuit. Conventional current is shown flowing from the positive terminal of a power supply to the negative terminal. Electrons in metal wires drift in the opposite direction.

I = Q ÷ t    and    Q = It

Current is measured in amperes (A), charge in coulombs (C), and time in seconds (s). One ampere means one coulomb passes each second.

Voltage is the energy transferred per coulomb: V = E ÷ Q. A battery gives energy to charges; components such as lamps transfer electrical energy into other forms, such as light and thermal energy. Charges are not used up in a circuit.

Resistance and Ohm’s law

V = IR    ·    R = V ÷ I    ·    I = V ÷ R

Resistance describes how strongly a component opposes current. In a metal wire, moving electrons collide with the metal ions in the lattice. These interactions transfer energy to the wire, heating it.

An ohmic conductor has a straight-line voltage-current relationship at constant temperature: resistance stays constant. A filament lamp is non-ohmic because its filament heats up as current increases, causing its resistance to rise. On a V–I graph with V on the vertical axis and I on the horizontal axis, the gradient is resistance.

Electrical power and energy

P = VI    ·    P = I²R    ·    P = V² ÷ R
E = Pt

Power is the rate of energy transfer. Energy is measured in joules (J), power in watts (W), and time in seconds. Choose the power formula that matches the values given.

Worked example. A 12 V lamp carries 0.50 A.
P = VI = 12 × 0.50 = 6.0 W.
In 15 minutes (900 s), E = Pt = 6.0 × 900 = 5400 J.

Series circuits

In a series circuit there is only one path for charge. The current is the same at every point, the supply voltage is shared between components, and resistances add.

Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + …

If another resistor is added in series, total resistance increases. With the same supply voltage, total current decreases. The voltage drops across the components add to the supply voltage.

Example. 4 Ω and 8 Ω resistors are in series across 12 V.
Rtotal = 4 + 8 = 12 Ω.
I = V/R = 12/12 = 1.0 A.
Voltage drops: 4 Ω gets 4 V; 8 Ω gets 8 V. Check: 4 + 8 = 12 V.

Parallel circuits

In a parallel circuit, components are on separate branches. Each branch has the same voltage as the supply. The total current is the sum of branch currents. Adding another branch gives charge another path, so total resistance decreases and total current increases.

1 ÷ Rtotal = 1 ÷ R₁ + 1 ÷ R₂ + …
Example. 6 Ω and 3 Ω resistors are connected in parallel across 12 V.
I₁ = 12/6 = 2 A; I₂ = 12/3 = 4 A.
Total current = 2 + 4 = 6 A.
Rtotal = V/I = 12/6 = 2 Ω.

Combination circuits

Simplify a mixed circuit one section at a time. First identify resistors that are clearly in parallel or series. Replace a parallel group with its equivalent resistance, then combine any remaining series resistors. Find the total current from the supply, then work backwards to find voltage drops and branch currents.

Two checks: current entering a junction equals current leaving it; and the voltage drops around a complete loop add to the supply voltage. Do not use the supply voltage across a resistor unless that resistor is directly connected across the supply.
Part 03 · Electromagnetism

Force on a current-carrying conductor

A current in a wire creates a magnetic field around the wire. If the wire is placed in another magnetic field, the fields interact and the wire can experience a force. This is called the motor effect.

F = BIL
  • F = force (N)
  • B = magnetic flux density (T)
  • I = current (A)
  • L = length of wire inside the magnetic field (m)

This form applies when the conductor is at right angles to the magnetic field. The force is zero if the wire is parallel to the field, or if there is no current. A larger field, current or wire length gives a larger force.

Use the left-hand motor rule to work out direction: first finger = magnetic field (north to south), second finger = conventional current, thumb = force/motion. Keep the three directions at right angles. In a motor coil, forces on opposite sides act in opposite directions and create a turning effect.

Worked example. A 0.12 m wire carries 3.0 A at right angles to a 0.40 T field.
F = BIL = 0.40 × 3.0 × 0.12 = 0.144 N.

Force on a moving charged particle

A moving charge behaves like a current. When it moves at right angles to a magnetic field, it experiences a sideways force.

F = Bqv

Here q is the magnitude of the charge (C) and v is speed (m s−1). The direction depends on the charge: use the right-hand/left-hand convention taught in class carefully, remembering that an electron’s force is opposite to the direction for a positive charge moving the same way.

If the velocity is perpendicular to the field, the force stays at right angles to the motion. It changes the direction of travel, not the speed, so the particle follows a circular path. The magnetic force does no work because it is perpendicular to the movement.

Bqv = mv² ÷ r    so    r = mv ÷ Bq

This gives the radius of the circular path for a particle moving perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field.

Part 04 · Electromagnetic induction

Producing an induced voltage

An induced voltage appears across a conductor when it moves through a magnetic field and cuts across magnetic field lines. If the circuit is complete, the induced voltage can drive a current. This is the basic idea behind a generator.

V = B L v

B is magnetic flux density (T), L is the length of conductor in the field (m), and v is speed (m s−1). This relationship applies when the conductor moves at right angles to the field and its length is at right angles to the field as in the standard straight-rod setup.

What affects the induced voltage?

  • Stronger magnetic field → greater induced voltage.
  • Longer conductor in the field → greater induced voltage.
  • Faster movement → greater induced voltage.
  • No cutting across the field lines → no induced voltage in this setup.

The direction of induced current can be found using the generator rule specified by your course. The induced effect acts to oppose the change that produces it (Lenz’s law). In a straight-rod question, carefully identify the field direction and motion direction before deciding the polarity.

Worked example. A 0.20 m rod moves at 5.0 m s−1 at right angles through a 0.30 T field.
V = BLv = 0.30 × 0.20 × 5.0 = 0.30 V.

Exam habits that earn clearer answers

  • Write the relationship before inserting numbers.
  • Convert every value to SI units first: mm → m; µC → C; minutes → seconds.
  • Show enough working for a marker to follow your method.
  • Include the correct unit with the final value.
  • For direction questions, state the direction in words and explain whether the charge is positive or negative.
  • For circuit explanations, describe the chain of cause and effect: resistance changes → total resistance changes → current changes (if supply voltage is fixed).
  • For magnetic diagrams, label field, current/velocity and force separately.
