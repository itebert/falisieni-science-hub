Welcome to Organic Chemistry!

Organic chemistry is the study of carbon-containing compounds. These substances are found in fuels, food, medicines, plastics and many everyday products.

Everyday example: The petrol in a car, the alcohol in hand sanitiser and the plastic in a drink bottle all involve organic chemistry.

How to study: Read the simple explanation, connect it to the everyday example, then learn the scientific vocabulary in bold.

1. The seven families of organic compounds

Organic compounds are grouped into families. Each family has a special part of its structure called a functional group. Think of functional groups like different uniforms: they help identify what kind of compound something is.

  • Alkanes: contain only carbon-carbon single bonds. Example: methane.
  • Alkenes: contain a carbon-carbon double bond. Example: ethene.
  • Alkynes: contain a carbon-carbon triple bond. Example: ethyne.
  • Alcohols: contain an –OH group. Example: ethanol.
  • Haloalkanes: contain a halogen such as chlorine or bromine attached to carbon.
  • Carboxylic acids: contain a –COOH group. Example: ethanoic acid in vinegar.
  • Amines: contain a nitrogen-containing group such as –NH₂.
Everyday connection: Vinegar contains ethanoic acid. Hand sanitiser commonly contains ethanol, an alcohol.
Exam tip: Learn to recognise each functional group from a structural formula. The functional group helps you identify the family.

2. Alkanes, alkenes and alkynes

These three families are hydrocarbons, meaning they contain only carbon and hydrogen.

  • Alkanes: single bonds only. General formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.
  • Alkenes: at least one double bond. For one double bond in an open chain, CₙH₂ₙ.
  • Alkynes: at least one triple bond. For one triple bond in an open chain, CₙH₂ₙ₋₂.
Think of bonds like connections: a single bond is one connection between two carbon atoms, a double bond is two, and a triple bond is three.

Examples: ethane C₂H₆, ethene C₂H₄ and ethyne C₂H₂.

Exam tip: These general formulae apply to open-chain compounds with one relevant bond type and no rings or additional multiple bonds.

3. Molecular, condensed and structural formulae

These are three different ways of showing the same molecule.

  • Molecular formula: tells you how many atoms of each element are present. Example: C₂H₆.
  • Condensed formula: groups atoms together. Example: CH₃CH₃.
  • Structural formula: shows how atoms are connected, including the bonds between carbon atoms.
Everyday connection: Think of a molecular formula as the shopping list, while a structural formula is the assembly diagram showing where everything goes.
Exam tip: Make sure every carbon has four bonds and every oxygen has two bonds in the usual structures studied at this level.

4. Naming organic compounds (IUPAC)

Organic names tell us about the carbon chain and functional group.

Step 1: Find the longest continuous carbon chain.

Step 2: Number the chain from the end that gives the important group or bond the lowest possible number, following naming priority rules.

Step 3: Identify branches and their positions.

Step 4: Identify the main functional group or bond and use the correct ending.

  • 1 carbon = meth-
  • 2 carbons = eth-
  • 3 carbons = prop-
  • 4 carbons = but-
  • 5 carbons = pent-

Common endings: -ane, -ene, -yne, -ol and -oic acid.

Example: CH₃CH₂OH has two carbon atoms and an alcohol group, so its name is ethanol.
Exam tip: Include position numbers when needed, such as but-1-ene and but-2-ene.

5. Primary, secondary and tertiary compounds

For alcohols and haloalkanes, primary, secondary and tertiary describe how many other carbon atoms are attached to the carbon holding the functional group.

  • Primary (1°): that carbon is attached to one other carbon.
  • Secondary (2°): attached to two other carbons.
  • Tertiary (3°): attached to three other carbons.
Think of neighbours: Count how many carbon neighbours the important carbon has. One neighbour means primary; two means secondary; three means tertiary.
Exam tip: Count carbon neighbours, not hydrogen atoms.

6. Constitutional isomers

Constitutional isomers have the same molecular formula but their atoms are connected in different ways.

Everyday connection: Imagine using the same number of building blocks to make two different buildings. The pieces are the same, but their arrangement is different.

For example, butane and 2-methylpropane both have the molecular formula C₄H₁₀, but their carbon skeletons are different.

Exam tip: Check that the molecular formula is identical before calling two structures isomers.

7. Geometric (cis/trans) isomers

Some alkenes can have different arrangements around a carbon-carbon double bond because the double bond restricts rotation.

  • Cis: the matching groups are on the same side.
  • Trans: the matching groups are on opposite sides.
Think of a fence: The double bond acts like a fixed fence between two sides. Groups cannot freely rotate around it.
Exam tip: Each carbon in the double bond must have two different groups attached for geometric isomerism to occur.

8. Solubility, melting point and boiling point

Solubility describes how well a substance dissolves in a solvent. Water is polar, so it generally dissolves polar substances better than non-polar substances.

Everyday example: Oil and water separate because oil is mostly non-polar while water is polar.

Boiling point is the temperature at which a liquid changes into a gas. Melting point is when a solid changes into a liquid.

Stronger attractions between molecules generally require more energy to overcome, giving higher boiling points. Longer carbon chains often have stronger dispersion forces and higher boiling points within a similar family.

Exam tip: Explain property differences by naming the intermolecular forces and linking their strength to the energy needed.

9. Reactions of alkanes

Alkanes are relatively unreactive. One important reaction is substitution, where a hydrogen atom is replaced by a halogen atom.

For example, methane can react with chlorine in ultraviolet light to form chloromethane and hydrogen chloride.

Think of swapping seats: One atom leaves its position and another takes its place.
Exam tip: This is a substitution reaction. Ultraviolet light is needed to initiate the reaction.

10. Addition reactions of alkenes

The carbon-carbon double bond in an alkene can open up so that new atoms attach to the two carbon atoms. This is called addition.

  • Hydrogen addition: hydrogen adds across the double bond.
  • Halogen addition: a halogen such as bromine adds across the double bond.
  • Hydrogen halide addition: HCl or HBr adds across the double bond.
  • Hydration: water adds across the double bond to form an alcohol.
Think of opening a zip: The double bond becomes a single bond, leaving room for new atoms to attach.
Exam tip: The double bond becomes a single bond in the addition product.

11. Testing for unsaturation

Unsaturated compounds contain carbon-carbon double or triple bonds.

Bromine water is commonly used to test for unsaturation. It is orange/brown and can become colourless when it reacts with an alkene or alkyne.

Think of a colour test: The colour change is a clue that a reaction has happened.
Exam tip: State the starting colour, final colour and what the observation means. A negative result does not prove the sample is an alkane unless other possibilities are considered.

12. Major and minor products

Some reactions can produce more than one product. The major product forms in the greater amount, while the minor product forms in the smaller amount.

For addition of an unsymmetrical reagent such as HBr to an unsymmetrical alkene, different products may form. The major product is often predicted using the stability of the reaction intermediate, depending on the reaction conditions and mechanism.

Think of two possible routes: One route is more favourable, so more of that product is made.
Exam tip: Draw both possible products where required and explain why one is favoured. Do not guess based only on the size of the molecule.

13. Oxidation of alkenes

Alkenes can react with oxidising agents. The product depends on the reagent and reaction conditions. In the course, you may study oxidation using potassium manganate(VII).

Think of a chemical change: An oxidising agent helps change the bonds and groups in a molecule, producing a different compound.
Exam tip: Learn the specific reagent, conditions and products your course requires. Do not assume every oxidation produces the same product.

14. Addition polymerisation

Many alkene molecules can join together to make a long-chain molecule called a polymer. The small starting molecules are called monomers.

Everyday example: Think of a long necklace. Each bead is a monomer, and the whole necklace is the polymer. Poly(ethene) is used in many plastic products.

The carbon-carbon double bond opens and forms links between monomers.

Exam tip: Draw the repeating unit in brackets and show bonds continuing through the brackets.

15. Oxidation of alcohols

Alcohols can be oxidised. The products depend on whether the alcohol is primary, secondary or tertiary and on the reaction conditions.

  • Primary alcohol: can be oxidised to an aldehyde, and further to a carboxylic acid.
  • Secondary alcohol: can be oxidised to a ketone.
  • Tertiary alcohol: does not readily oxidise under the usual conditions studied at this level.
Think of a transformation: The alcohol group is changed into a different functional group by oxidation.
Exam tip: Identify the type of alcohol first, then decide the product and conditions.

16. Substitution reactions of alcohols

An alcohol can react so that its –OH group is replaced by a halogen. This produces a haloalkane.

Think of swapping labels: The –OH group is replaced by a halogen atom, changing the compound's family.
Exam tip: Identify what group is replaced and what new group takes its place. Learn the reagent and conditions required by your course.

17. Substitution reactions of haloalkanes

In a haloalkane, the halogen can be replaced by another group. For example, reaction with aqueous hydroxide ions can produce an alcohol.

Everyday connection: Think of replacing one puzzle piece with another while keeping the rest of the picture.
Exam tip: Distinguish aqueous conditions, which can favour substitution, from alcoholic conditions, which can favour elimination.

18. Elimination reactions

Elimination removes atoms or groups from a molecule and forms a double bond. For example, a haloalkane can lose a hydrogen atom and a halogen atom to form an alkene.

Think of removing two pieces: When the pieces leave, a double bond can form between the carbon atoms.
Exam tip: Substitution replaces a group. Elimination removes groups and forms a double bond. Know the conditions that favour each reaction.

19. Acid-base reactions of carboxylic acids and amines

Carboxylic acids can donate a hydrogen ion (H⁺), so they act as acids. Amines can accept a hydrogen ion using the lone pair on nitrogen, so they act as bases.

Everyday example: Vinegar contains ethanoic acid. Amines are found in many important biological molecules and medicines.

An acid and a base can react to form an ionic salt. The exact salt depends on the acid and base involved.

Exam tip: Show the transfer of H⁺ and identify the ions or salt formed.

20. Reaction pathways: putting it all together

A reaction pathway is a sequence of chemical reactions that changes one organic compound into another.

For each arrow, ask:

  1. What is the starting compound?
  2. What functional group does it contain?
  3. What reaction type is needed?
  4. What reagent and conditions are required?
  5. What product will form?
Think of a journey: A starting compound is your starting point. Each reaction is a step along the route. The final product is your destination.
Exam tip: Practise identifying the reaction type, reagents, conditions and product for every arrow. Check that atoms are conserved.
