Start with the big picture

Gene expression is the use of information in a gene to make a functional product, usually a polypeptide that becomes part of a protein. Proteins affect how cells work and, through cell processes, help produce an organism's characteristics.

DNA gene→mRNA→Polypeptide→Protein→Characteristic

This page follows that chain, then explores how mutations and environmental conditions can change the final phenotype.

1. DNA, RNA and the genetic instructions

Nucleotides: the building blocks

DNA and RNA are nucleic acids. Both are long molecules made by joining many smaller units called nucleotides. Each nucleotide contains a phosphate group, a sugar and a nitrogen-containing base.

DNA sugar

Deoxyribose

RNA sugar

Ribose

DNA bases

Adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), guanine (G)

RNA bases

Adenine (A), uracil (U), cytosine (C), guanine (G)

Nucleotides connect through their sugars and phosphates, forming a repeating sugar-phosphate backbone. The bases extend from this backbone and carry the sequence information.

Complementary base pairing

The matching is specific. A base on one strand determines which base can fit opposite it. In DNA, the two strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases.

Try it: If a DNA strand reads A–T–C–G, its partner reads T–A–G–C. The two sequences are complementary.

Direction of DNA strands

Each DNA strand has a direction, described as 5′ to 3′. The two strands in a DNA molecule run in opposite directions, so they are antiparallel. This arrangement allows the bases to line up correctly.

Coding strand and template strand

  • The template strand is the DNA strand that is read when an mRNA copy is made.
  • The coding strand is the other DNA strand. Its base sequence matches the mRNA sequence except that DNA uses T where RNA uses U.

A template strand is T A C G G T A C. To build the coding strand, use DNA base pairing. To make mRNA, pair RNA bases with the template.

There are four A–T pairs and four C–G pairs. Total hydrogen bonds = (4 × 2) + (4 × 3) = 20 hydrogen bonds.

Exam reminder: Check which strand the question gives you. mRNA is made from the template strand, not the coding strand. Also remember that RNA contains U, not T.

2. How the genetic code is read

The genetic code links groups of bases to amino acids. It is read in sets of three bases.

TermFound onMeaning
DNA tripletDNAA group of three bases in the DNA sequence.
CodonmRNAA three-base unit that specifies an amino acid or a translation signal.
AnticodontRNAA three-base sequence that pairs with an mRNA codon.

A tRNA anticodon binds to the complementary codon on mRNA. This matching helps ensure that the tRNA brings the amino acid specified by the codon.

Why does the code use three bases?

There are four possible RNA bases. If a code used two bases at a time, there would be only 4² = 16 combinations. That is not enough to represent around 20 amino acids. Three bases create 4³ = 64 possible codons, enough to code for amino acids and include start/stop signals.

Degeneracy and redundancy

The genetic code is described as degenerate because more than one codon can specify the same amino acid. For example, the four codons CCU, CCC, CCA and CCG all specify proline.

This means a base change does not always alter the amino acid. If a changed codon still represents the same amino acid, the polypeptide sequence may stay exactly the same. This feature is also described as redundancy in the code.

Important distinction: Degeneracy describes the fact that several codons can mean the same amino acid. Redundancy describes the extra coding capacity that results. This can reduce the effect of some substitutions.

Start and stop codons

  • AUG is the usual start codon. It signals the start of translation and codes for methionine.
  • Stop codons signal the end of translation. They do not add an amino acid to the chain.

If a mutation changes a codon into a stop codon before the normal end, translation stops too soon. The polypeptide is shorter than expected and may not function.

Using a codon table

  1. If you are given a DNA template sequence, transcribe it into mRNA first.
  2. Divide the mRNA into groups of three, starting at the correct reading point.
  3. Use the codon table to identify each amino acid.
  4. Do not add an amino acid for a stop codon.
Example: Template DNA TAC AAA CGG ATT gives mRNA AUG UUU GCC UAA. The codons specify methionine, phenylalanine and alanine, followed by a stop signal. The polypeptide has three amino acids.
Exam reminder: Read the heading on the codon table. Most tables use mRNA codons, but a question may provide a special table based on DNA template triplets.

3. Protein synthesis: transcription and translation

Protein synthesis uses the information in DNA to assemble a chain of amino acids. It has two main stages, and they happen in different places.

TranscriptionTranslation
Where?NucleusRibosome in the cytoplasm
Information readDNA template strandmRNA codons
Main resultmRNAPolypeptide

Stage A — Transcription

  1. The section of DNA containing the gene unwinds and the two strands separate as hydrogen bonds are broken.
  2. The template strand is used as a guide.
  3. Free RNA nucleotides pair with the exposed DNA bases using complementary base pairing. RNA uses U instead of T.
  4. An enzyme joins the RNA nucleotides into an mRNA strand.
  5. The mRNA separates from the DNA and passes out of the nucleus through a nuclear pore. The DNA strands join back together.
Why make mRNA? DNA stays protected in the nucleus. mRNA is a portable copy of one gene that can carry the instructions to a ribosome.

Stage B — Translation

  1. The mRNA attaches to a ribosome.
  2. The ribosome reads the mRNA in codons, beginning at the start codon.
  3. tRNA molecules bring particular amino acids. Each tRNA has an anticodon that pairs with a complementary mRNA codon.
  4. The ribosome joins the amino acids in the order specified by the mRNA, creating a polypeptide chain.
  5. When a stop codon is reached, translation ends and the polypeptide is released.
  6. The polypeptide folds into a particular three-dimensional shape. It can then function as part of a protein.

mRNA and tRNA do different jobs

mRNA (messenger RNA)tRNA (transfer RNA)
Carries a copy of the gene's code from the nucleus to a ribosome.Delivers a specific amino acid to the ribosome.
Its codons are read by the ribosome.Its anticodon pairs with the matching mRNA codon.
Usually a single, relatively long strand for the gene being expressed.Short RNA folded into a shape that allows it to carry an amino acid.

Enzymes and the ribosome's catalytic activity help the stages proceed. Conditions that affect enzymes, such as temperature and pH, can therefore affect protein synthesis.

Do not mix these up: DNA replication copies DNA before cell division. Transcription uses one DNA strand to make an RNA copy of a gene. Translation reads mRNA to build a polypeptide.

The cause-and-effect chain

DNA base order→mRNA codons→Amino-acid order→Protein folding→Shape + function

This chain is central to gene expression. A change to the DNA sequence can affect the mRNA, the amino-acid sequence, the protein's folding and ultimately what the protein can do.

4. Why proteins matter

A gene generally contains the instructions for making one polypeptide. A polypeptide is a chain of amino acids. Some proteins consist of one polypeptide; others contain several chains, each encoded by its own gene.

Shape is linked to function

The order of amino acids influences how a polypeptide folds. Folding gives the protein its particular three-dimensional shape. The shape determines which molecules it can interact with and what role it can perform.

Structural proteins

Build, support or strengthen parts of an organism. Collagen supports connective tissues such as tendons and skin. Keratin is found in hair, nails and the outer layer of skin.

Catalytic proteins

Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions. Their active sites have shapes that allow particular substrates to bind.

Enzymes control many metabolic reactions. If an enzyme is missing or has a changed active site, the reaction it normally speeds up may slow down or stop. This can affect the substances a cell makes and therefore its phenotype.

Exam wording: Link a change in amino-acid sequence to altered folding, then altered protein shape and function. Avoid jumping straight from “DNA changed” to “the organism looks different”.

5. Metabolic pathways and phenotype

A metabolic pathway is a series of enzyme-controlled reactions inside a cell. The product made in one reaction becomes the starting substance (substrate) for the next reaction. Different genes provide the instructions for the enzymes at different steps.

Substance A— enzyme 1 →Substance B— enzyme 2 →Substance C

The final products of pathways can influence visible characteristics. For example, if a pathway makes a pigment, the pigment present can affect the colour shown by an organism.

What if a gene in the pathway is changed?

  1. A mutation changes the base sequence of a gene.
  2. The mRNA made from that gene may have a different codon sequence.
  3. The polypeptide may have a changed amino-acid sequence and fold differently.
  4. The enzyme may have an altered active site or may not be produced.
  5. The reaction controlled by that enzyme may fail, blocking the pathway at that point.
  6. The substrate before the blocked step can build up, while later products may not be made.
  7. The change in products can alter the phenotype.
Example — pigment pathway: Imagine a pathway where a colourless substance P is changed into yellow Q, then blue R. If enzyme 2 does not work, Q can accumulate while R is not produced. The flower may look yellow rather than blue.

Albinism

Melanin is a pigment found in skin, hair and eyes. Its production involves a metabolic pathway with enzymes. In some forms of albinism, a gene variant results in an enzyme that does not work properly. The pathway is disrupted and much less melanin is produced, affecting pigmentation.

A pathway can also be interrupted by conditions

  • Missing materials: a reaction cannot proceed if its substrate or a required mineral helper is unavailable. For example, nitrogen deficiency can limit chlorophyll production in plants.
  • Inhibitors: some substances can bind to enzymes and reduce their activity.
  • Unsuitable temperature or pH: the enzyme may not maintain the shape needed to catalyse the reaction.

In these situations, the DNA sequence can remain unchanged. When the environmental problem is corrected, the pathway may work again.

Finding the blocked step from evidence

Look for the substance that has accumulated and the product that is missing. If Q is abundant but R is absent in the P → Q → R pathway, the P-to-Q step is working, but the Q-to-R step is blocked. This points to enzyme 2 and the gene that codes for it.

For a full explanation: identify the faulty gene, name the enzyme it codes for, explain how the enzyme's active site is affected, and state both outcomes: the earlier substrate builds up and the later product is not formed.

6. Gene mutations and mutagens

A gene mutation is a permanent change in the DNA base sequence within a gene. Since DNA sequence helps determine the amino-acid sequence of a polypeptide, a mutation can change the protein and possibly the phenotype.

Substitution

A substitution replaces one base with another. The number of bases stays the same, so the reading groups usually remain in the same positions. The altered codon can have one of three broad outcomes:

TypeWhat changes?Possible result
SilentThe new codon still specifies the same amino acid.Polypeptide sequence is unchanged; there may be no effect on the phenotype.
Mis-senseThe new codon specifies a different amino acid.One amino acid is changed. The effect depends on how that change affects folding and function.
Non-senseThe new codon becomes a stop codon.Translation ends early, producing a shorter polypeptide that may not function.

Insertion and deletion

An insertion adds a base; a deletion removes a base. If a single base is added or removed, the groups of three after that point are shifted. This is a frameshift mutation.

Because the reading frame has moved, many downstream codons can change. The amino-acid sequence after the mutation may be very different, and the polypeptide may be a different length if a stop signal appears early or the original stop signal is lost.

MutationTypical impact
SubstitutionMay change no amino acids, one amino acid, or create an early stop.
Single-base insertion/deletionCan change most codons after the mutation because the reading frame shifts.
Think of a sentence: Read letters in groups of three. Removing one letter changes how the later letters are grouped. A frameshift does something similar to the codon reading frame.

Mutagens

A mutagen is a factor that increases the frequency at which mutations occur. Examples include ultraviolet (UV) radiation, ionising radiation such as X-rays, and certain chemicals in tobacco smoke. A mutagen increases the chance of mutations; it does not select a particular change that must happen.

Mutation or environmental response? A mutation changes DNA and therefore the genotype. An environmental effect can change how a characteristic appears without changing the DNA sequence.

Worked comparison

Original template: TAC GGA TTC ACT → mRNA: AUG CCU AAG UGA. The last codon is a stop, so the chain contains three amino acids.

  • If a base substitution changes the second mRNA codon from CCU to UCU, one amino acid changes. This is a mis-sense substitution; the chain length can remain the same.
  • If one base is deleted, the reading frame after that position shifts. Several later codons can be altered, so the resulting polypeptide is much more likely to lose its normal shape and function.
Exam tip: Compare the scale of the effect. A substitution may affect one codon; a one-base insertion or deletion can alter the reading of many codons after it. Then connect the altered amino-acid sequence to folding and function.

7. How the environment can affect phenotype

Genotype means the genetic information an organism carries. Phenotype means the characteristics that can be observed or measured. Phenotype is influenced by genotype and the environment.

Genotype + environmental conditions → phenotype

An environmental factor can affect the phenotype without changing the DNA sequence. The genotype stays the same, but gene activity, enzyme function or the availability of materials can change.

Three ways conditions can influence a characteristic

1. Altering gene expression

Conditions can affect whether a gene is switched on or how actively it is transcribed. More mRNA can lead to more of the protein being made.

2. Affecting enzyme activity

The enzyme may already be present, but temperature, pH or an inhibitor can affect whether it works effectively.

3. Limiting raw materials

A pathway may slow or stop if a required substrate or mineral is not available.

Examples to understand

ExampleHow the environment affects the phenotype
Plant grown in light or darknessLight affects the expression of genes involved in chlorophyll production. A plant grown without light may be pale; in light it can become green.
Himalayan rabbitThe pigment-producing enzyme works better in cooler body regions. Fur is darker on cooler extremities and pale on warmer parts.
Hydrangea flowersSoil conditions, including pH and aluminium availability, influence flower colour. The plant's DNA sequence does not need to change.
Flamingo colourPigments in food contribute to the pink colour of feathers.
Human heightGenes influence a possible range, while nutrition and other conditions during growth affect the height reached.
